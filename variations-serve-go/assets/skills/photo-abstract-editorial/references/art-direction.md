## Source analysis

Treat the supplied photograph as the sole factual source. Work through this sequence internally, without outputting analysis text:

DECONSTRUCT → SELECTIVE PRESERVATION → ABSTRACT / DISTILL → RECONSTRUCT

This is neither style transfer nor photo vectorization. Before composing the motif, identify three to six decisive facts from:

- relative scale and left/right or upper/lower position;
- horizontal/vertical axes, curves, movement, and pauses;
- intervals, repetition, overlap, depth, and asymmetry;
- light/dark hierarchy, color roles, and negative space.

Remove surface textures, perspective detail, background noise, and low-information ornament. Retain relationships, not surface detail. The motif should read first as a sparse abstract composition and only later recall the particular photograph.

## Abstraction system

Choose one primary mark family and at most two supporting families.

Primary families:

- flat or slightly organic color masses;
- soft irregular circles;
- arcing or tapered strokes;
- short bars or stacked bands;
- simplified architectural masses.

Supporting families:

- fine structural axes;
- one primary and one subordinate vertical anchor when the source contains decisive vertical structure;
- one or two narrow negative-space cuts or slight horizontal band offsets;
- restrained human ink marks;
- a small amount of irregular repetition.

Map subjects conservatively:

- Landscapes, water, horizons, and roads: use two to four irregular bands and one dark structural line. If a pole, mast, or tower is compositionally decisive, translate it into exactly two unequal irregular vertical anchors: one taller and slightly thicker, one shorter and thinner. Keep both subordinate to the horizontal bands and omit crossarms, wires, latticework, and complete infrastructure outlines. Otherwise use one or two negative gaps or band offsets.
- Landmark architecture: retain one to three identity cues such as a taper, arch, spire, eave line, or characteristic negative space; omit windows and surface ornament.
- Organic groups such as balloons, trees, clouds, or lights: use overlapping soft masses without realistic highlights or internal patterns.
- People: render each person as one continuous, irregular short vertical ink mark or lightly tapered color block; join head, shoulders, and body as one form. Create crowd rhythm through height, width, intervals, tilt, and front-to-back overlap. Never draw separate circular heads, limbs, faces, or clothing.
- Railings, roads, horizons, and shores: compress into one or two fine horizontal axes with a few irregular interruptions.
- Small representative objects: retain two or three planar cues based on scale and position; omit realistic volume and material.

Every important mark must answer a visible fact. Preserve the minimum necessary recognizability rather than replicating objects. Let adjacent marks vary slightly in scale and position, with pauses that create an observed natural rhythm rather than an infographic or vector icon. Avoid regularized spacing, invented symmetry, generic icons, literal tracing, complete illustration, and decorative filler.

## Adaptive composition

Keep the full photograph and join it directly to a flat panel. Choose the arrangement by source orientation: for a landscape or near-square source, stack the panel below the photograph; for a portrait source (aspect at most 0.8), place the panel to the right of the photograph — never stack a portrait photo above a panel on a tall skinny canvas. Do not use frames, tape, shadows, torn edges, mockups, or collage effects.

The panel always matches the rendered photograph in size: for stacked layouts it has the same width and height as the photo (the canvas is the photo doubled downward); for side-by-side layouts (portrait sources) it spans the full height of the photo with the same width (the canvas is the photo doubled rightward). Never crop the photograph aggressively.

Use one editorial grid without forcing every element onto identical numeric margins. For lower-left layouts, place the motif with a small structural inset in the left part of the panel and give the title a deeper bottom inset; keep the motif low on the panel and preserve internal intervals, overlap, center of gravity, and asymmetry. Judge margins optically, not by forced equality.

Use a uniform warm ivory panel by default (#E8E1D5). The panel itself must contain no gradient, lighting variation, shadow, glow, vignette, seam, grain, paper texture, watercolor wash, noise, stain, haze, or scan artifact. Abstract marks may have slightly natural edges and handmade irregularity, but never rely on material noise to create atmosphere. Create atmosphere only with whitespace, distance, pauses, asymmetry, scale contrast, limited marks, and a restrained palette.

## Panel layout

Place the motif low on the panel — lower-left by default, or at a center or asymmetric position when source relationships support it. Default scale:

- motif width: about 30%–42% of panel width;
- motif height: usually no more than 28%–34% of panel height;
- keep about 65%–80% clean whitespace.

Adapt by subject:

- slender horizons, bridges, roads, or horizontal crowds may reach 45%–68% of panel width while staying low;
- landmark towers or vertical subjects may grow taller but must not fill the panel;
- compact organic groups stay gathered; never scatter them across the panel;
- do not shrink the motif into a generic small icon merely to satisfy a percentage.

Preserve the motif's internal scale relationships, directions, intervals, occlusion, repetition, center of gravity, and asymmetry as one whole.

## Color system

Extract colors only from the photograph and reduce saturation and quantity. Use one dominant role, one dark structural role, one light/neutral role, and at most one or two small accents. Accent colors must be important colors that truly exist in the photo and may appear only on a few key marks. Prefer oxidized, mineral, or ink-like versions of source colors; never add unsupported neon or complementary colors.

## Title and typography

Create one original English title, preferably two to five words, grounded in visible subject, light, time, motion, or spatial relationships. The title must be faithful (corresponds to a real subject relationship or visual fact), clear (concise and natural, no awkward literal translation), and elegant (poetic with resonance, without melodrama or affected depth). Choose one naming direction:

- light or time entering a space;
- a relationship or dialogue between two subjects;
- a brief appearing, floating, receding, or pausing;
- a metaphor distilled from the photo's colors, structural axes, or movement;
- an original compound word plus a short qualifier.

Avoid travel advertising, literal place descriptions, photography jargon, generic Memory, Dream, or Moment, and unrelated grand narratives. Decide one final title internally and render exactly that; never render candidate lists or alternatives.

Add a three-to-seven-word subtitle only when it contributes a new idea. Place the title only on the panel, below or beside the motif, never in the photo area, inside the motif, at the lower right, or on the canvas edge:

- lower-left aligned when the motif spreads horizontally or the overall weight leans right, with a small safe distance from the left and lower edges;
- bottom centered when the motif is centered, the axis is clear, or the composition is symmetrical — aligned with the motif's central axis but never touching it.

Prefer Bodoni or another high-contrast editorial serif, with Baskerville or Garamond as fallbacks; use a composed book serif for architectural subjects and a fine humanist serif for light, nature, or lyrical subjects. Natural whole-run kerning rather than uniform character spacing. One short connector may be clearly smaller in the matching italic face; do not stylize multiple words. Use a dark, restrained subject color drawn from the photo or motif — such as deep blue-gray, dark green, wine red, deep purple, or charcoal — legible on the panel without defaulting to pure black; a subtitle uses a lighter or less saturated color from the same family. Keep all text inside the panel, away from the motif and canvas edge.

## Acceptance criteria

The final work contains exactly:

1. one rendering of the full source photograph, faithful to the source;
2. one uniform flat panel;
3. one traceable abstract motif;
4. one English title;
5. at most one optional subtitle.

Reject any result containing extra words, letters inside the motif, numbers, dates, place labels, color swatches, legends, signatures, logos, watermarks, redrawn or invented photo content, aggressive crop, filters, outpainting, vector tracing, recognizable traced objects, dense decoration, candidate title lists, or non-uniform panel texture.
